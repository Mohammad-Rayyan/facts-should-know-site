Octopuses have nine brains and three hearts but still live only 5 years

Published November 21, 2026

Octopuses possess nine brains and three hearts, yet most species live only one to five years before dying shortly after reproducing. This remarkable creature, capable of solving puzzles and using tools, has a lifespan shorter than most pets despite having more brainpower than a dog.

Nine brains working in concert

Each of an octopus's eight arms contains its own mini-brain called a ganglion, housing about 40 million neurons that allow the arm to function semi-independently. The central brain contains around 180 million neurons total, coordinating the entire system. Some scientists debate whether these truly constitute nine separate brains or one distributed brain system, since the arm ganglia aren't fully autonomous but do function with remarkable independence. Severed octopus arms can continue moving and reacting to stimuli even when completely detached from the body.

Three hearts pumping blue blood

Two of the octopus's hearts pump blood exclusively to the gills, while a third pumps it to the rest of the body. Their blood appears blue because it uses copper-based hemocyanin instead of the iron-based hemoglobin found in humans. This cardiovascular system would be the envy of any athlete, yet it powers a body programmed for an extremely short existence.

Programmed to die young

After mating, female octopuses stop eating entirely and waste away while guarding their eggs for months. This death spiral is controlled by optic gland hormones that essentially program their demise. Laboratory studies have shown that removing these optic glands makes octopuses live longer and skip the self-destructive sequence.

Lifespan varies by species, with giant Pacific octopuses living three to five years while some smaller species survive only six months. Evolution chose rapid reproduction over longevity, optimizing these intelligent creatures for passing on genes rather than accumulating experience or developing culture.

What could have been

Octopuses rank among the most intelligent invertebrates on Earth, capable of recognizing individual humans and demonstrating problem-solving abilities that rival many vertebrates. If humans had octopus lifespans proportional to our complexity, we would die before finishing elementary school despite possessing all our mental abilities. The question lingers: if octopuses lived as long as parrots or elephants, would they develop culture and technology as humans did?

Nature gave the octopus extraordinary intelligence and physiology but programmed it to die young, proving that evolution optimizes for reproduction, not longevity.
