The Cutty Sark was reportedly clocked at about 17 knots under sail

Published November 25, 2026

Cutty Sark was launched at Dumbarton on 22 November 1869. Under sail in good winds, she was reportedly capable of about 17 knots, or roughly 31 km/h. That made her one of the fastest sailing ships of her era, though the figure needs some care.

A ship built for tea

Scott & Linton built Cutty Sark at Dumbarton, Scotland. The design was by Hercules Linton, and the owner was John Willis. She had a composite hull, an iron frame planked in wood, and she was designed for the China tea trade.

Tea clippers raced to bring each year's new tea crop from China to London. Speed was the whole point of the design.

Where the 17 knots comes from

The figure of about 17 knots is commonly given, but it does not come from instrument trials. It comes from reported logged speeds. Sources also vary on the exact number.

It is a peak, not an average. Her speed in strong winds was exceptional, but she depended on the weather.

Fast, but not always first

Cutty Sark was not the fastest tea clipper in every race. In the often-cited 1872 race with Thermopylae, she lost her rudder and fell behind.

You will sometimes read that she was faster than many steamships. There is no precise sourced comparison behind that. Steamers of the time varied widely in speed, and the claim holds only for her peak speed in strong winds. Over a whole voyage, a clipper's pace depended on the wind and on a longer route around the Cape of Good Hope. Steamers could hold a steady speed and take the shorter Suez route.

Launched as the trade was changing

The Suez Canal opened in 1869, the same year Cutty Sark was launched. It gave steamships a shorter route to the East. She was among the last of the tea clippers, built just as steam and the canal were changing the trade.

She made only eight tea voyages, from 1870 to 1877, before moving to the Australian wool trade. She is now preserved at Greenwich, London, and is run by Royal Museums Greenwich as a museum ship.

Cutty Sark was reportedly capable of about 17 knots in good winds, a peak from logged speeds rather than a measured average. It made her one of the fastest sailing ships of her day, not the winner of every race.
