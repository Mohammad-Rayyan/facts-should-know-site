The Moon rang for nearly an hour after Apollo 12's spent lander hit it

Published November 26, 2026

On 20 November 1969, the empty ascent stage of an Apollo lunar module struck the Moon about 70 km from a seismometer left by its crew. The instrument recorded vibrations for roughly 55 minutes. That was far longer than scientists expected for an impact of that size.

A crash planned in advance

Apollo 12 astronauts Pete Conrad and Alan Bean deployed a Passive Seismic Experiment in November 1969. It was part of the ALSEP station, set up near their landing site in the Ocean of Storms.

After the crew docked with the command module Yankee Clipper, the ascent stage of the lunar module Intrepid was released. It was sent into the lunar surface on purpose. The aim was to give the seismometer a known impact to record.

A signal that built slowly and faded slowly

The impact point was roughly 70 km from the seismometer. The signal did not arrive as a single sharp jolt. It built up slowly, then faded over about 55 minutes.

The duration is usually given as about 55 minutes, so describing it as nearly an hour is a rounding. Scientists had expected something much shorter.

Why the vibrations lingered

Seismologists explained the long signal with two things working together. One is intense scattering of seismic waves in the Moon's fractured, heterogeneous outer layers. The other is very low attenuation, which is associated with the Moon being extremely dry.

This is the standard interpretation, but it is an interpretation. Scattering in the fractured upper layers and low intrinsic attenuation both contribute, and the exact split between them is not a settled number.

Not quite a bell

Popular versions say the Moon rang like a bell. That is a loose image. The recording shows a gradual rise and a long decay of scattered waves, not a clear tone.

The long signal still told scientists a great deal about the structure of the lunar crust, and it came from a piece of spacecraft that had no other job left to do.
