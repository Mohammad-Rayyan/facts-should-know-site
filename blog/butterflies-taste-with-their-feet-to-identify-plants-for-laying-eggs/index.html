Butterflies taste with their feet to identify plants for laying eggs

Published November 22, 2026

A female butterfly landing on a leaf often drums it with her front legs. She is not resting. The feet carry taste receptors, and the drumming is how she checks whether the plant is a safe place to lay her eggs.

Taste organs on the feet

The taste organs are hair-like structures called sensilla, also known as chemosensilla. They sit on the tarsi, the last segments of the legs. Their job is to detect chemicals on the surface of a leaf.

So when a butterfly stands on a plant, she is also sampling it.

Why she drums

Females often drum or scratch the leaf with their forelegs. This breaks the leaf's surface and releases plant chemicals, which the receptors on the feet can then sense.

The drumming gives the feet something to taste beyond whatever sits on the outside of the leaf.

Why the choice matters

Many butterfly species lay eggs only on a narrow group of host plants. The reason is practical. Caterpillars often eat only a few plant species and cannot travel far, so they need to hatch on food they can eat.

The foot receptors respond to both stimulants and deterrents in plants. In the swallowtail Papilio xuthus, researchers have identified specific compounds that trigger egg-laying through the foreleg tarsi.

One cue among several

Foot taste is not the whole story. Butterflies also use sight and smell to find host plants, and they approach a plant using those senses first. Taste on the feet is the final check before laying.

The popular version of this fact is simplified. Butterflies do not taste only with their feet, since the mouthparts and antennae also sense chemicals. Details differ by species, and the best-studied cases involve females choosing egg-laying sites. Males have foot taste receptors too, and so do other insects such as flies.

A female butterfly drums a leaf to taste it with her feet, and that contact check is the last step before she decides whether to lay eggs there.
