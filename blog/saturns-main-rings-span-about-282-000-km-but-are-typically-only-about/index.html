Saturn's main rings span about 282,000 km but are typically only about 10 meters thick

Published November 22, 2026

Saturn's main rings reach out about 282,000 km, roughly 175,000 miles. They are typically only about 10 meters thick, about 30 feet. That is a structure wider than a planet's whole neighborhood and flatter than almost anything you could build.

The numbers side by side

NASA describes the main rings as extending up to about 282,000 km from the planet. The same description puts their typical thickness at about 10 meters.

That is a ratio of tens of thousands to one. In places the rings may be thinner still.

What the rings are made of

The rings are made mostly of water-ice particles. Some are tiny grains. Others are chunks as large as houses or mountains.

So the thin layer is not a sheet or a film. It is a crowd of separate pieces, spread out in a very flat plane.

Wide, but not heavy

The ring system's total mass is small for its size. Cassini measurements indicated a mass about 0.4 times that of Mimas.

The main rings are divided into sections labeled A, B, C and D. The Cassini Division, a gap, separates the A and B rings.

Where the figures need care

The 10 meter figure is typical, not exact. Thickness varies by ring and by location, and some sources give tens of meters. Ring-edge waves and vertical structures can make some regions thicker.

The 282,000 km figure refers to the main rings only. Faint outer rings, such as the Phoebe ring, extend much farther.

You may have seen the comparison to a sheet of paper. It is a proportional analogy, and whether it holds depends on the scale you choose.
