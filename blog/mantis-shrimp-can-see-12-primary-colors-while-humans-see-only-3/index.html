Mantis shrimp can see 12 primary colors while humans see only 3

Published November 24, 2026

Mantis shrimp have 12 to 16 types of photoreceptor cells for color vision. Humans have three. By that count the shrimp should win easily, but when they were tested on telling similar colors apart, they did worse than people.

A big gap in the cell count

The compound eyes of a mantis shrimp hold 12 to 16 types of photoreceptor cells. Human color vision relies on three types of cone cells.

For years, that difference was taken to mean mantis shrimp must see color far better than we do. The popular version says they see 12 primary colors. That claim is misleading. Having more types of photoreceptor does not by itself mean sharper color vision.

What happened when they were tested

A 2014 study published in Science trained mantis shrimp to recognize specific colors. The animals could only distinguish colors that were 15 to 25 nanometers apart in wavelength.

Humans can distinguish colors just 1 to 4 nanometers apart. When the task is telling one shade from a close neighbor, humans are significantly better.

Built for speed, not precision

Researchers at the University of Queensland found that mantis shrimp appear to use their photoreceptors as a recognition system rather than a comparison system. That allows faster but coarser color processing.

The advantage of their visual system seems to be how quickly they can recognize a color, not how finely they can separate two similar ones.

Where their eyes do go further

Mantis shrimp can detect polarized light and ultraviolet light, as well as visible wavelengths. Those are visual abilities beyond human perception.

So their eyes do outdo ours, but in dimensions other than fine color discrimination. Polarization is one of the properties they handle well.

Twelve to sixteen photoreceptor types did not make mantis shrimp better than humans at telling similar colors apart. Their strengths are fast recognition and the detection of polarized and ultraviolet light.
